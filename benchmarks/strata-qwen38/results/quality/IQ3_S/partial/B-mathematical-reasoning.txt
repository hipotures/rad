To find the expected total number of rolls until two consecutive rolls have the same value, we can model this problem using states and linear equations (Method 1) or by analyzing the probability distribution of the stopping time (Method 2).

### Method 1: State-Based Approach (First-Step Analysis)

Let $E$ be the expected total number of rolls needed to get two consecutive identical values, starting from scratch.

We can define the state of the process based on the history of the rolls. However, a simpler way is to condition on the first roll.

Let $X$ be the random variable representing the total number of rolls.
The process starts with the first roll. Let's call the value of the first roll $R_1$.
After the first roll, we are in a state where we have one "active" value that we need to match. Let $E_{match}$ be the expected *additional* number of rolls needed to get a match, given that we have just rolled a value and are waiting for the next roll to match it.

However, it is often clearer to define $E$ as the expected number of rolls from the start.
Let's break down the process:
1.  We always make the first roll. This costs 1 roll.
2.  After the first roll, we look at the second roll.
    *   With probability $p = \frac{1}{6}$, the second roll matches the first. The process stops. Total rolls = 2.
    *   With probability $q = \frac{5}{6}$, the second roll does not match the first. The process continues.

If the second roll does not match the first, we have effectively "reset" the problem, but we have already used 2 rolls. The new "current" value is the second roll. The expected number of *additional* rolls needed from this point is the same as the expected number of rolls needed to get a match starting from a single previous roll.

Let's refine the definition:
Let $E$ be the expected number of rolls to get two consecutive matches starting from a state where we have **no** previous roll (start of game).
Let $M$ be the expected number of *additional* rolls needed to get a match, given that we have **one** previous roll (i.e., we are waiting for the next roll to match the current one).

**Step 1: Analyze state $M$**
From state $M$ (we have a current value $v$):
- We roll once. This costs 1 roll.
- With probability $\frac{1}{6}$, the new roll matches $v$. We stop. Additional rolls = 1.
- With probability $\frac{5}{6}$, the new roll does not match $v$. We are now in state $M$ again (with a new current value). The expected additional rolls from here is still $M$.

So, the equation for $M$ is:
$$ M = 1 + \frac{1}{6}(0) + \frac{5}{6}(M) $$
Wait, let's be precise. $M$ is the expected *additional* rolls.
$$ M = 1 \cdot \left(\frac{1}{6} \cdot 0 + \frac{5}{6} \cdot M\right) + 1 \text{ (for the roll itself)} $$
Actually, standard form:
$$ M = 1 + \frac{5}{6}M $$
(The "1" accounts for the roll we just made. If it matches (prob 1/6), we add 0 more. If it doesn't (prob 5/6), we add $M$ more.)

Solving for $M$:
$$ M - \frac{5}{6}M = 1 $$
$$ \frac{1}{6}M = 1 $$
$$ M = 6 $$

So, if you have just rolled a number, you expect to roll 6 more times (including the matching one) to get a pair? No, $M$ is the expected number of rolls *from the state where you have a previous roll*.
Let's re-verify.
If $M=6$, that means on average, it takes 6 rolls to get a match *after* having a previous roll.
Let's check the logic:
Expected rolls to get a specific match (geometric distribution with $p=1/6$) is $1/p = 6$.
Yes, once you have a "target" value, the number of rolls to hit that target is geometric with mean 6. This includes the successful roll. So $M=6$ is correct.

**Step 2: Analyze state $E$ (Start of game)**
At the start, we have no previous roll.
1. We must make the first roll. Cost = 1.
2. After this first roll, we are in state $M$ (we have a current value and are waiting for a match).
3. The expected additional rolls from this point is $M$.

So,
$$ E = 1 + M $$
$$ E = 1 + 6 = 7 $$

**Conclusion from Method 1:** The expected total number of rolls is 7.

### Method 2: Probability Distribution of the Stopping Time

Let $N$ be the random variable for the total number of rolls. We want to find $E[N] = \sum_{n=2}^{\infty} n \cdot P(N=n)$.

The process stops at roll $n$ if:
1. The first $n-1$ rolls do not contain any consecutive matches.
2. The $n$-th roll matches the $(n-1)$-th roll.

Let's calculate $P(N=n)$.

**For $n=2$:**
Roll 1 is anything. Roll 2 must match Roll 1.
$$ P(N=2) = \frac{1}{6} $$

**For $n=3$:**
Roll 1 is anything.
Roll 2 must NOT match Roll 1 (prob $\frac{5}{6}$).
Roll 3 must match Roll 2 (prob $\frac{1}{6}$).
$$ P(N=3) = 1 \cdot \frac{5}{6} \cdot \frac{1}{6} = \frac{5}{36} $$

**For $n=4$:**
Roll 1 is anything.
Roll 2 must NOT match Roll 1 (prob $\frac{5}{6}$).
Roll 3 must NOT match Roll 2 (prob $\frac{5}{6}$).
Roll 4 must match Roll 3 (prob $\frac{1}{6}$).
$$ P(N=4) = 1 \cdot \frac{5}{6} \cdot \frac{5}{6} \cdot \frac{1}{6} = \frac{25}{216} $$
