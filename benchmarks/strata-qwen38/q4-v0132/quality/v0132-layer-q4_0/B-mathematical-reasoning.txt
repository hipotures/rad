To find the expected total number of rolls until two consecutive rolls have the same value, we can model this problem using states and linear equations (Method 1) or by analyzing the probability distribution of the stopping time (Method 2).

### Method 1: State-Based Approach (First-Step Analysis)

Let $E$ be the expected number of *additional* rolls needed to achieve two consecutive matching values, given that we have just rolled a die and the previous roll does not match the current one (or we are at the start).

However, it is more precise to define states based on the history of the last roll.
Let $E_0$ be the expected number of *additional* rolls needed to stop, given that we have not yet started rolling (or the previous roll is irrelevant because we need a pair).
Actually, a simpler state definition is:
Let $E$ be the expected number of *additional* rolls needed to get a match, given that we have just rolled a number and the previous roll was different (or we are at the very beginning where no previous roll exists to match against).

Let's refine the states:
1.  **State $S$ (Start/No Match Context)**: We are about to roll. We need to roll until we get a match with the *previous* roll. But at the very start, there is no previous roll.
    Let's define $E$ as the expected total number of rolls from the beginning.

Let's use a recursive approach based on the outcome of the next roll.
Let $E$ be the expected number of rolls needed to get two consecutive identical values starting from scratch.

Consider the first roll:
- We must roll at least once. Let this roll be $X_1$.
- Now we are in a state where we have a "current" value $X_1$, and we need to roll until we get $X_1$ again.
- Let $E_{match}$ be the expected number of *additional* rolls needed to get a value equal to the immediately preceding roll.

So, $E = 1 + E_{match}$.

Now, let's analyze $E_{match}$. We are in a state where the previous roll was $v$. We roll again.
- With probability $p = \frac{1}{6}$, the new roll is $v$. We stop. The number of additional rolls used is 1.
- With probability $q = \frac{5}{6}$, the new roll is not $v$. Let the new roll be $w$. Now the "previous roll" for the next step is $w$. We have used 1 roll, and we are back in the same situation: we need to match the current roll $w$. The expected *additional* rolls from this point is still $E_{match}$.

So, we can write the equation for $E_{match}$:
$$ E_{match} = 1 \cdot \frac{1}{6} + (1 + E_{match}) \cdot \frac{5}{6} $$

Let's solve for $E_{match}$:
$$ E_{match} = \frac{1}{6} + \frac{5}{6} + \frac{5}{6}E_{match} $$
$$ E_{match} = 1 + \frac{5}{6}E_{match} $$
$$ E_{match} - \frac{5}{6}E_{match} = 1 $$
$$ \frac{1}{6}E_{match} = 1 $$
$$ E_{match} = 6 $$

This $E_{match}$ represents the expected number of rolls *after* the first roll to get a match.
The total expected number of rolls $E$ includes the first roll:
$$ E = 1 + E_{match} = 1 + 6 = 7 $$

**Answer via Method 1:** 7

### Method 2: Probability Distribution of the Stopping Time

Let $N$ be the random variable representing the total number of rolls until two consecutive rolls are the same.
The minimum value for $N$ is 2 (e.g., rolls are 1, 1).

Let's find the probability $P(N = k)$ for $k \ge 2$.
For the process to stop exactly at roll $k$, the following must happen:
1.  The first $k-1$ rolls must **not** contain any consecutive matches.
2.  The $k$-th roll must match the $(k-1)$-th roll.

Let's analyze the sequence of rolls $X_1, X_2, \dots, X_k$.
- $X_1$ can be any value (probability 1).
- For $i = 2$ to $k-1$, we require $X_i \neq X_{i-1}$. Since the die is fair, given $X_{i-1}$, there are 5 outcomes out of 6 that are different. So, $P(X_i \neq X_{i-1}) = \frac{5}{6}$.
- For the stopping condition at $k$, we require $X_k = X_{k-1}$. Given $X_{k-1}$, there is 1 outcome out of 6 that matches. So, $P(X_k = X_{k-1}) = \frac{1}{6}$.

Note that the condition "no consecutive matches in the first $k-1$ rolls" implies:
$X_2 \neq X_1$, $X_3 \neq X_2$, ..., $X_{k-1} \neq X_{k-2}$.
There are $k-2$ such inequalities for the indices $2$ through $k-1$.

So, the probability that the process stops exactly at roll $k$ is:
$$ P(N = k) = \underbrace{1}_{X_1} \cdot \underbrace{\left(\frac{5}{6}\right)^{k-2}}_{\text{no matches in } X_2 \dots X_{k-1}} \cdot \underbrace{\frac{1}{6}}_{\text{match at } X_k} $$
$$ P(N = k) = \frac{1}{6} \left(\frac{5}{6}\right)^{k-2} \quad \text{for } k \ge 2 $$

Let's verify this is a valid probability distribution. The sum of probabilities should be 1.
$$ \sum_{k=2}^{\infty} P(N=k) = \sum_{k=2}^{\infty} \frac{1}{6} \left(\frac{5}{6}\right)^{k-2} $$
Let $j = k-2$. As $k$ goes from 2 to $\infty$, $j$ goes from 0 to $\infty$.
$$ \sum_{j=0}^{\infty} \frac{1}{6} \left(\frac{5}{6}\right)^{j} = \frac{1}{6} \cdot \frac{1}{1 - 5/6} = \frac{1}{6} \cdot \frac{1}{1/6} = 1 $$
The distribution is valid.

Now, we calculate the expected value $E[N]$:
$$ E[N] = \sum_{k=2}^{\infty} k \cdot P(N=k) = \sum_{k=2}^{\infty} k \cdot \frac{1}{6} \left(\frac{5}{6}\right)^{k-2} $$

Let $r = \frac{5}{6}$. Then $P(N=k) = \frac{1}{6} r^{k-2}$.
$$ E[N] = \frac{1}{6} \sum_{k=2}^{\infty} k r^{k-2} $$

Let $j = k-2$, so $k = j+2$.
$$ E[N] = \frac{1}{6} \sum_{j=0}^{\infty} (j+2) r^j $$
$$ E[N] = \frac{1}{6} \left[ \sum_{j=0}^{\infty} j r^j + 2 \sum_{j=0}^{\infty} r^j \right] $$

We use the standard geometric series formulas for $|r| < 1$:
1.  $\sum_{j=0}^{\infty} r^j = \frac{1}{1-r}$
2.  $\sum_{j=0}^{\infty} j r^j = \frac{r}{(1-r)^2}$

Substitute $r = \frac{5}{6}$:
1.  $1-r = \frac{1}{6}$
2.  $\sum_{j=0}^{\infty} r^j = \frac{1}{1/6} = 6$
3.  $\sum_{j=0}^{\infty} j r^j = \frac{5/6}{(1/6)^2} = \frac{5/6}{1/36} = \frac{5}{6} \cdot 36 = 30$

Now plug these back into the expression for $E[N]$:
$$ E[N] = \frac{1}{6} [ 30 + 2(6) ] $$
$$ E[N] = \frac{1}{6} [ 30 + 12 ] $$
$$ E[N] = \frac{1}{6} [ 42 ] $$
$$ E[N] = 7 $$

**Answer via Method 2:** 7

### Conclusion

Both methods yield the same result.

1.  **State-Based Method**: Defined the expected additional rolls needed to match the previous roll as $E_{match} = 6$. Adding the initial roll gives $1 + 6 = 7$.
2.  **Distribution Method**: Derived the probability mass function $P(N=k) = \frac{1}{6}(\frac{5}{6})^{k-2}$ and calculated the expectation using series summation, resulting in 7.

The expected total number of rolls is **7**.
