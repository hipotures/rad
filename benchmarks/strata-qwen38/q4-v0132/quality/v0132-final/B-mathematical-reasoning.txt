To find the expected total number of rolls until two consecutive rolls have the same value, we can model this problem using states and linear equations (Method 1) or by analyzing the probability distribution of the stopping time (Method 2).

### Method 1: State-Based Approach (First-Step Analysis)

Let $E$ be the expected number of *additional* rolls needed to achieve two consecutive matching values, given that we have just rolled a die and the previous roll does not match the current one (or we are at the start).

However, it is more precise to define states based on the history of the last roll.
Let $E_0$ be the expected number of *additional* rolls needed to finish the game, given that we have just started (no previous roll to compare to).
Let $E_1$ be the expected number of *additional* rolls needed to finish the game, given that we have just rolled a value $v$, and the previous roll was *not* $v$ (or simply, we have a "current" value $v$ and we are waiting for the next roll to either match $v$ or establish a new "current" value).

Actually, a simpler state definition is:
Let $E$ be the expected number of *additional* rolls needed to get a match, given that we have a "current" value from the previous roll.

**Step 1: Analyze the first roll.**
The first roll always happens. It establishes a "current" value. It cannot form a pair with a previous roll because there is no previous roll. So, we have used 1 roll. We are now in a state where we have a current value, and we need to roll again to see if it matches.

Let $E$ be the expected number of *additional* rolls needed to get a match, given that we currently hold a value from the previous roll.

**Step 2: Set up the equation for $E$.**
From the state where we have a current value $v$:
1. We roll the die once. This counts as 1 roll.
2. With probability $p = \frac{1}{6}$, the new roll matches $v$. The game ends. The number of additional rolls used is 1.
3. With probability $q = \frac{5}{6}$, the new roll does not match $v$. The game does not end. However, the new roll becomes the new "current" value. We are back in the same state (having a current value and waiting for a match), but we have already spent 1 roll. So, the expected *remaining* rolls is $E$.

Thus, the equation for $E$ is:
$$ E = 1 \cdot \left(\frac{1}{6}\right) + (1 + E) \cdot \left(\frac{5}{6}\right) $$

Let's solve for $E$:
$$ E = \frac{1}{6} + \frac{5}{6} + \frac{5}{6}E $$
$$ E = 1 + \frac{5}{6}E $$
$$ E - \frac{5}{6}E = 1 $$
$$ \frac{1}{6}E = 1 $$
$$ E = 6 $$

So, $E=6$ is the expected number of *additional* rolls needed after the first roll has established a current value.

**Step 3: Calculate Total Expected Rolls.**
The total number of rolls $T$ consists of:
1. The first roll (which establishes the initial current value).
2. The additional rolls required to find a match ($E$).

$$ T = 1 + E $$
$$ T = 1 + 6 = 7 $$

**Conclusion from Method 1:** The expected total number of rolls is 7.

### Method 2: Probability Distribution of the Stopping Time

Let $N$ be the random variable representing the total number of rolls until the first pair of consecutive identical values appears.
The minimum number of rolls is 2 (e.g., 1, 1).
The maximum is infinite.

Let's determine the probability $P(N = k)$ for $k \ge 2$.

For the game to end exactly at roll $k$:
1. The first $k-1$ rolls must **not** contain any consecutive matches.
2. The $k$-th roll must match the $(k-1)$-th roll.

Let's analyze the sequence of rolls $X_1, X_2, \dots, X_k$.
- $X_1$ can be any value (probability 1).
- For $i = 2$ to $k-1$, we require $X_i \neq X_{i-1}$. Since the die is fair, given $X_{i-1}$, there are 5 outcomes out of 6 that are different. So, $P(X_i \neq X_{i-1}) = \frac{5}{6}$.
- For the $k$-th roll, we require $X_k = X_{k-1}$. Given $X_{k-1}$, there is 1 outcome out of 6 that matches. So, $P(X_k = X_{k-1}) = \frac{1}{6}$.

Therefore, the probability that the game ends exactly at roll $k$ is:
$$ P(N = k) = \underbrace{1}_{X_1} \cdot \underbrace{\left(\frac{5}{6}\right)^{k-2}}_{\text{no matches in } X_2 \dots X_{k-1}} \cdot \underbrace{\frac{1}{6}}_{\text{match at } X_k} $$
Note: The term $(\frac{5}{6})^{k-2}$ comes from the $k-2$ transitions from $X_1 \to X_2$ up to $X_{k-2} \to X_{k-1}$ where no match occurred.
Wait, let's verify the indices.
- $N=2$: $X_1$ (any), $X_2=X_1$. Prob = $1 \cdot \frac{1}{6}$. Formula: $(\frac{5}{6})^0 \cdot \frac{1}{6} = \frac{1}{6}$. Correct.
- $N=3$: $X_1$ (any), $X_2 \neq X_1$, $X_3 = X_2$. Prob = $1 \cdot \frac{5}{6} \cdot \frac{1}{6}$. Formula: $(\frac{5}{6})^1 \cdot \frac{1}{6}$. Correct.
- $N=k$: Prob = $\frac{1}{6} \left(\frac{5}{6}\right)^{k-2}$.

This is a geometric distribution shifted by 1. Let $j = k-1$. Then $k = j+1$.
The probability mass function is defined for $k \ge 2$.

The expected value $E[N]$ is:
$$ E[N] = \sum_{k=2}^{\infty} k \cdot P(N=k) $$
$$ E[N] = \sum_{k=2}^{\infty} k \cdot \frac{1}{6} \left(\frac{5}{6}\right)^{k-2} $$

Let $r = \frac{5}{6}$.
$$ E[N] = \frac{1}{6} \sum_{k=2}^{\infty} k r^{k-2} $$

Let $j = k-2$. Then $k = j+2$. When $k=2, j=0$.
$$ E[N] = \frac{1}{6} \sum_{j=0}^{\infty} (j+2) r^j $$
$$ E[N] = \frac{1}{6} \left[ \sum_{j=0}^{\infty} j r^j + 2 \sum_{j=0}^{\infty} r^j \right] $$

We use the standard geometric series sums for $|r| < 1$:
1. $\sum_{j=0}^{\infty} r^j = \frac{1}{1-r}$
2. $\sum_{j=0}^{\infty} j r^j = \frac{r}{(1-r)^2}$

Substitute $r = \frac{5}{6}$:
$$ 1-r = 1 - \frac{5}{6} = \frac{1}{6} $$

Calculate the sums:
1. $\sum_{j=0}^{\infty} r^j = \frac{1}{1/6} = 6$
2. $\sum_{j=0}^{\infty} j r^j = \frac{5/6}{(1/6)^2} = \frac{5/6}{1/36} = \frac{5}{6} \cdot 36 = 30$

Now substitute back into the expression for $E[N]$:
$$ E[N] = \frac{1}{6} [ 30 + 2(6) ] $$
$$ E[N] = \frac{1}{6} [ 30 + 12 ] $$
$$ E[N] = \frac{1}{6} [ 42 ] $$
$$ E[N] = 7 $$

**Conclusion from Method 2:** The expected total number of rolls is 7.

### Final Answer

Both methods yield the same result.

The expected total number of rolls is **7**.
